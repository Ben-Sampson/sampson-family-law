Custody and Relocation in Oklahoma

The best-interests standard, the 2024 safety amendments, how custody orders change, and the rules for moving with a child.

By the Sampson Family Law editorial team (fictional) · 7 min read · General information, not legal advice.

Custody decisions in Oklahoma are guided by the child's best interests. A few specific rules shape how that standard is applied at the start of a case, when a parent wants to change an order, and when a parent wants to move. Citations are listed with links at the end of this article.

The best-interests standard

Courts decide custody based on "the physical and mental and moral welfare of the child." Custody may go to either parent or to both jointly. A parent who asks for joint custody must file a parenting plan covering living arrangements, support, medical care, schooling, and visitation (43 O.S. § 109(A)–(C)).

Safety comes first

Since November 1, 2024, a court finding of child abuse, domestic violence (including coercive control), stalking, or harassment creates a rebuttable presumption that custody with the parent who committed it is not in the child's best interests. The victim's safety is a primary factor (43 O.S. § 109(I)). Courts must also consider risk factors such as sex-offender registration and certain criminal convictions, some of which create their own presumptions (43 O.S. § 112.2).

Changing an existing order

The parent seeking a change must show a permanent, substantial, and material change in circumstances that directly affects the child, and that the child would be substantially better off with the change (Gibbons, 1968 OK 77, ¶ 12).

Moving with a child

A move of more than 75 miles for 60 days or more is a "relocation." The parent planning to move must mail notice at least 60 days in advance, with the new address, the reasons for the move, and a proposed new visitation schedule. The other parent has 30 days after receiving notice to object, or the move is allowed (43 O.S. § 112.3(A), (C), (G)). If there is an objection, the moving parent must show the move is made in good faith. The objecting parent must then show the move is not in the child's best interests, and the court weighs eight statutory factors (43 O.S. § 112.3(J)–(K); Mahmoodjanloo, 2007 OK 32, ¶ 12). A change in the visitation schedule alone does not justify blocking a move (Kaiser, 2001 OK 30, ¶¶ 29, 33).

Practical steps

Follow your current order while any request is pending, keep communication with the other parent factual and in writing, and calendar notice deadlines carefully. Missing the 30-day objection window can decide a relocation dispute.

Statutes and cases

Statutes

  1. 43 O.S. § 109Sets the best-interests standard, joint custody plans, and (since Nov. 1, 2024) a presumption against custody with a parent found to have committed abuse or domestic violence.
  2. 43 O.S. § 112.2Lists mandatory custody considerations and rebuttable presumptions, such as sex-offender registration and certain convictions.
  3. 43 O.S. § 112.3Governs relocation notice, the 30-day objection period, the best-interest factors, and the good-faith burden-shifting framework.

Cases

  1. Gibbons v. Gibbons, 1968 OK 77, ¶ 12, 442 P.2d 482Okla. 1968. Modifying custody requires a permanent, substantial, and material change of conditions and proof that the child would be substantially better off.
  2. Mahmoodjanloo v. Mahmoodjanloo, 2007 OK 32, ¶ 12, 160 P.3d 951Okla. 2007. Under § 112.3(K), the relocating parent need only prove good faith; the burden then shifts to the objecting parent to show the move is not in the child's best interest.
  3. Kaiser v. Kaiser, 2001 OK 30, ¶¶ 18, 29, 33, 23 P.3d 278Okla. 2001. Decided before § 112.3: a custodial parent has a presumptive right to relocate, and altered visitation alone does not justify restricting a move.
