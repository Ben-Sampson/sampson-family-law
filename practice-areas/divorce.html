Divorce

Thoughtful guidance through the legal and financial decisions that come with ending a marriage.

Ending a marriage with clarity

A divorce is two things at once: a legal case and one of the hardest personal transitions most people ever face. The legal side asks a district court to dissolve the marriage and decide how property, debts, support, and parenting will be handled. The personal side involves grief, uncertainty, and dozens of decisions that seem to arrive all at once. Good representation treats both seriously.

In Oklahoma, divorce is governed largely by Title 43 of the Oklahoma Statutes. Before filing, at least one spouse generally must have been a resident of the state for six months. Most divorces proceed on the no-fault ground of incompatibility, which means neither spouse has to prove wrongdoing to end the marriage.

Property, debt, and support

Oklahoma divides marital property under an equitable distribution approach. “Equitable” means fair under the circumstances, which is not always the same as an exact fifty-fifty split. Separating marital property from separate property (such as assets owned before the marriage or received by gift or inheritance) is often the most important financial work in a case, especially where accounts have been mixed over time.

Support alimony may be available where one spouse has a demonstrated need and the other has the ability to pay. Retirement accounts, business interests, and the family home all require careful valuation and, often, specialized court orders to divide correctly. A settlement that looks fair on paper can carry hidden tax or liquidity problems, so we review every proposal with long-term consequences in mind.

Agreed or contested

Many divorces settle. When spouses can agree, the case can move through a negotiated decree with far less cost and conflict. When there are minor children, Oklahoma generally applies a waiting period of ninety days after filing before a decree may be entered, and parents may be required to complete a parenting education program.

When agreement is not possible, a contested case involves temporary orders, financial disclosures, discovery, and possibly trial. Our approach is the same either way: understand the facts thoroughly, give you an honest assessment, and pursue the resolution that best protects your interests and your children.

A fair settlement is not just about today's balance sheet. It is about whether you can rebuild comfortably in the years ahead.

FAQ

Divorce questions, answered

How long does a divorce take in Oklahoma?

It depends on whether the case is agreed or contested and whether there are minor children. Agreed cases without children can sometimes conclude quickly; cases with children are generally subject to a ninety-day waiting period, and contested cases can take many months.

Do I have to prove my spouse did something wrong?

Usually not. Most Oklahoma divorces use the no-fault ground of incompatibility. Conduct can still matter in some financial or parenting questions.

Will everything be split fifty-fifty?

Not necessarily. Oklahoma uses equitable distribution of marital property, which aims for fairness rather than a strict equal split. Separate property is generally treated differently.

Can we use one lawyer for both of us?

A lawyer can represent only one spouse. The other spouse may choose to represent themselves or hire separate counsel, and mediation is another way to reach agreement.
